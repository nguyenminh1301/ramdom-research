\section*{TL;DR}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item The ostrich effect is a psychological syndrome that occurs when one faces a difficult problem but does not want to confront it.
    \item Based on behavioral psychology, avoiding information when feeling uncomfortable is a normal behavior in cognitively aware adults.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Findings}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Psychological syndrome
    \item Avoidance
    \item Cognitive bias
\end{itemize}

\section*{Analysis / Personal assessment}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item When do we become an "ostrich"? This psychology arises when you receive unwanted information and either refuse to accept it or want to evade it.
    \item For example, an investor wakes up to see their stock portfolio turn "red." The immediate onset of depression and disappointment triggers the "ostrich" nature. The investor gives up and lets things slide, much like an ostrich burying its head in the sand waiting for danger to pass, instead of selling shares to recoup some capital or finding a solution. Another simple example: today you ate more than usual, and the food was more expensive; instead of checking your account balance, you choose to turn a blind eye and continue the bad habit. US studies indicate that 20\% of people who sign up for a weight loss program have never actually stepped on a scale to measure their weight.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{How to avoid avoiding?}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Acknowledge that there are reasons to avoid negative information. The information may contain negative, stressful, threatening, or psychologically damaging content, etc. However, ignoring information deepens ideological and behavioral polarization.
    \item Think carefully about why you might avoid something — you may be prioritizing short-term comfort over long-term benefits. Recognizing this can help reframe uncomfortable information into something useful and valuable.
\end{itemize}
